# Press Release

**Ebisu Kaiten-Zushi Licenses Kaiten IQ, an RFID Plate-Tracking System, to Conveyor-Belt Sushi Restaurants Nationwide**
Restaurant operators get automatic freshness enforcement and per-plate checkout tallying, replacing hand-counted colors and paper timers.

TORRANCE, CALIFORNIA -- March 2, 2027 -- Ebisu Kaiten-Zushi, an 18-location kaiten-zushi (conveyor-belt sushi) chain based in Torrance, California, is licensing Kaiten IQ, the RFID-based plate-tracking platform it built for its own restaurants, to other conveyor-belt sushi operators starting today.

Kaiten-zushi operators track plate freshness with a colored plate system and a wall clock or paper timer: a server notes what time a plate went on the belt and pulls it after a set window, by memory or a sticky note. When staff get pulled onto other tasks, or a shift turns over mid-service, a plate sits past its freshness window, and a health inspector documents it. At checkout, a server counts each color by hand or scans barcode stickers one at a time, which slows table turns and produces disputes when a plate gets miscounted or slides into the wrong stack.

Kaiten IQ tags every plate with an RFID chip. When kitchen staff commission a menu item onto plates, Kaiten IQ records the item and a "born on" timestamp automatically instead of relying on a handwritten note. Fixed antennas along the belt track every plate in real time and flag one the moment it reaches its category's freshness window, so floor staff pull it without watching a clock. At checkout, a guest's stacked plates go on a reader once, and Kaiten IQ tallies the bill by plate color and price tier in seconds instead of a server counting by hand.

"A paper timer only works if the person who set it is still watching it an hour later," said Aiko Tanaka, founder and CEO of Ebisu Kaiten-Zushi. "We built Kaiten IQ because we got tired of finding out a plate sat too long after the fact instead of the moment it happened."

"Our servers used to spend the first minute of every checkout counting plates twice because the first count never matched the second," said James Okafor, owner of a six-location conveyor-belt sushi chain piloting Kaiten IQ. "Now the reader does it once and the guest sees the same number we do."

Ebisu Kaiten-Zushi is opening a pilot licensing program to conveyor-belt sushi operators today, ahead of general licensing availability. [LICENSING_INQUIRY_LINK] has details for operators who want to apply.

## Internal FAQ

**What's the addressable market?**
[TAM_ESTIMATE]: roughly 400 conveyor-belt sushi operators in the United States running 5 to 50 locations each, most still using a colored-plate-and-timer system with no chip-level tracking. A second, smaller segment is single-location operators considering their first multi-unit expansion who want freshness controls in place before they scale staffing across locations they can't personally watch.

**What's the competitive landscape?**
No competitor targets kaiten-zushi specifically. General-purpose restaurant RFID inventory vendors track back-of-house stock, not plates circulating on a belt in front of guests, and generic checkout hardware doesn't distinguish a plate's price tier by color. The alternative most operators use today is the colored-plate-and-timer system itself: no incremental cost, but no real-time enforcement and no automated tally either.

**What are the unit economics?**
[UNIT_ECONOMICS_DETAIL]: hardware and licensing cost per location depend on belt length (more antennas) and average daily plate volume (more tags in circulation). Ebisu Kaiten-Zushi's own 18 locations average [DAILY_PLATE_VOLUME] plates commissioned per location per day; per-tag cost drops as an operator's total tag order grows, so a 5-location operator's per-unit economics look different from a 50-location operator's.

**What are the top risks?**
Tag durability under repeated commercial dishwasher cycles and constant belt friction is the biggest technical risk: a tag that fails early erodes an operator's trust in freshness enforcement, since a plate showing no timestamp is indistinguishable from one that was never at risk. A second risk: some operators, especially long-tenured staff at family-owned locations, resist replacing a system they've run by hand for years, even after seeing shorter checkout times in a demo. The pilot exists to surface both before general licensing availability, not after.

**What needs to be true for this to succeed?**
The pilot with three licensee locations needs to show two things: that tags survive Ebisu Kaiten-Zushi's own 18-location commissioning and dishwasher volume at a failure rate operators find acceptable, and that checkout time actually drops enough for an independent operator to justify the licensing cost against their own labor savings. Neither is confirmed outside Ebisu Kaiten-Zushi's own restaurants yet.
