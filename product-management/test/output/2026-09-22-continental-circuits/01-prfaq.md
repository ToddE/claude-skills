# Press Release

**Continental Circuits Opens a U.S. Factory to Build a Smart-Hub That Discloses Where Every Chip Came From**
Security integrators serving government and critical-infrastructure customers get a Matter-compatible hub built only on U.S.- and EU-fabricated chips, with a country-of-origin record for every shipped unit.

ZANESVILLE, OHIO -- November 10, 2027 -- Continental Circuits opens its first factory today, an assembly plant in Zanesville, Ohio, that builds the Trustpoint Hub, a Matter-compatible security gateway for homes, school buildings, and small facilities. The hub's chips, the applications processor, the Wi-Fi and Zigbee radio, the cellular backup modem, and the secure element, come from a fab in the United States or the European Union.

Security dealers who sell into school districts, municipal buildings, and small utilities increasingly work with customers who can't install networking or surveillance equipment from vendors on the FCC's Covered List. Today, verifying where a hub's components actually come from means calling a distributor, waiting on an answer that may not exist, and hoping it holds up if a procurement office asks again next year. Most consumer smart-hubs don't publish chip-level sourcing at all.

The Trustpoint Hub ships with a country-of-origin attestation for every production lot, generated from the same supplier qualification records Continental Circuits uses to approve chip vendors before they're allowed into the bill of materials. A procurement office can review the attestation directly instead of taking a distributor's word for it. The hub itself covers the same ground as other Matter hubs: door, window, and motion sensors, a Wi-Fi 6 radio, and an LTE backup connection that keeps the system reachable if the building's internet goes down.

"A lot of companies talk about supply chain resilience without being able to name a single fab," said Maya Reyes, founder and CEO of Continental Circuits. "We can name all of them, because we chose them one at a time and we keep the paperwork."

"Our last hub deployment took four months longer than it should have because we couldn't get straight answers about component sourcing for a school district contract," said a security integrator piloting the Trustpoint Hub. "This is the first hub where the documentation shows up before I have to ask for it."

Continental Circuits is running a pilot production phase with three integrator design partners starting in the second quarter of 2027, ahead of today's general availability. [GETTING_STARTED_LINK] has details for security dealers who want to become authorized resellers.

**What's the addressable market?**
[TAM_ESTIMATE]: the initial segment is security integrators serving K-12 districts, municipal government buildings, and small critical-infrastructure operators (water utilities, credit unions) subject to sourcing restrictions under NDAA Section 889 (the federal law barring certain foreign-made networking and surveillance gear from government-funded contracts) and the related FCC Covered List. A second, smaller segment is security-conscious consumers and small businesses willing to pay a premium for disclosed sourcing. Both segments need sizing against actual district and municipal procurement volume, not estimated from total smart-hub market size.

**What's the competitive landscape?**
SmartThings, Ring, and Aqara-based white-label hubs dominate the category on price and ecosystem reach, but none publish chip-level country-of-origin documentation. A handful of "Made in USA" security hardware vendors exist at a much higher price point aimed at enterprise and defense customers; Continental Circuits' wedge is offering documented sourcing at a price a school district or small integrator can actually procure.

**How does chip supplier qualification work, and how long does it take?**
A part number reaches the bill of materials only after its supplier's fab location, export-control classification, and manufacturing capacity have been verified and approved. [SUPPLIER_QUALIFICATION_TIMELINE] per new supplier, covering documentation review, sample testing, and an approved-vendor-list entry. Dropping in an unqualified supplier to cover a shortage isn't an option without re-running this process, which is slower than how most electronics manufacturers respond to a supply shock.

**What are the top risks?**
Chip allocation risk is the biggest one: the secure element and RF chips this hub depends on are allocation-constrained industry-wide, and Continental Circuits' sourcing requirement removes the largest pool of alternate suppliers (Chinese-fabricated equivalents) that a less restrictive competitor could fall back on during a shortage. A second risk: tooling and certification lead times (UL, FCC) are fixed costs that don't compress under schedule pressure the way software timelines sometimes do, so a slip in supplier qualification pushes the launch date directly instead of getting absorbed elsewhere.

**What needs to be true for this to succeed?**
The pilot with three integrator design partners needs to prove two things: that qualified U.S./EU chip supply can sustain production at the pilot's volume without a shortage, and that procurement offices will actually accept Continental Circuits' attestation format instead of requiring their own separate audit. Either one failing changes the plan; both are unverified today.
